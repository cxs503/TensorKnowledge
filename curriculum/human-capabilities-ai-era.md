# Human Capabilities in the AI Era

**Status:** foundational draft, v0.1.0  
**Role in TensorKnowledge:** capability framework connecting the project's vision, six knowledge domains, learning pathways, tutorials, and assessment model.

## 1. The central question

When AI systems can retrieve information, generate explanations, write code, analyze data, and execute complex workflows, what should people learn?

The wrong answer is to compete with machines at every task, or to assume that human learning is no longer necessary. The better question is:

**What capabilities allow a person to understand the world, set worthwhile directions, work with powerful systems, judge outcomes, take responsibility, and create meaningfully?**

This framework is about capabilities people should develop—not a prediction that machines can never perform particular tasks. Technical capabilities change. Human agency, accountability, relationships, and the social legitimacy of decisions remain important even as tools become more capable.

## 2. A capability architecture

The framework has four mutually reinforcing layers. They are not a rigid sequence or a hierarchy of human worth.

### Layer A — Foundations for understanding

These capabilities let a person form and revise a usable picture of reality.

1. **Perception and observation:** attend to phenomena, distinguish observation from interpretation, notice anomalies, and ask what may be missing.
2. **Language and conceptualization:** define terms, clarify questions, explain ideas, and move between everyday and technical language.
3. **Quantitative and formal reasoning:** use number, measurement, logic, mathematics, probability, and computation to make relationships explicit.
4. **Evidence and epistemic judgment:** evaluate sources, distinguish fact from inference and value, calibrate confidence, and recognize uncertainty.
5. **Mental modeling:** construct, compare, test, and revise representations of systems while understanding their assumptions and limits.

### Layer B — Inquiry and transformation

These capabilities turn understanding into investigation and useful change.

6. **Problem framing:** identify what matters, define boundaries, expose assumptions, and turn vague concerns into answerable questions.
7. **Causal and experimental reasoning:** formulate hypotheses, identify alternative explanations, design tests, and interpret results without overclaiming.
8. **Systems and interdisciplinary thinking:** reason about interactions, feedback, emergence, scale, and knowledge transfer across domains.
9. **Design and making:** turn needs and ideas into artifacts, processes, explanations, or experiences; prototype, test, and improve them.
10. **Creativity and discovery:** generate alternatives, combine ideas, explore uncertainty, and pursue original questions or forms.

### Layer C — Agency in a world of agents

These capabilities help people direct and evaluate increasingly capable AI systems and human teams.

11. **AI and tool fluency:** understand what tools can and cannot do; choose tools; provide context; inspect provenance; test outputs; and manage data and security.
12. **Delegation and orchestration:** decompose goals, specify interfaces and acceptance criteria, allocate work, coordinate parallel effort, and integrate results.
13. **Critical evaluation and verification:** seek counterevidence, reproduce key results, inspect failure modes, and distinguish plausible output from demonstrated performance.
14. **Learning agility:** identify knowledge gaps, learn unfamiliar fields, practice deliberately, reflect on errors, and update mental models.
15. **Collaborative communication:** explain intent, listen, negotiate meaning, give and receive critique, and work across expertise and cultural differences.

### Layer D — Responsibility, meaning, and civilization

These capabilities orient action toward human and social purposes.

16. **Ethical and civic judgment:** identify stakeholders, rights, duties, power, harms, distributional effects, and competing values.
17. **Responsibility and accountability:** own consequential choices, disclose uncertainty and assistance, maintain auditability, and provide remedies when things go wrong.
18. **Self-direction and purpose:** choose goals, sustain attention, reflect on values, and decide what is worth doing.
19. **Care and relationship:** understand other people's needs and perspectives, build trust, cooperate, and sustain communities.
20. **Long-horizon and civilizational thinking:** consider institutions, ecological limits, intergenerational effects, and how today's systems shape future possibilities.

## 3. Three kinds of learning

A balanced education should cultivate all three:

- **Know:** concepts, facts, methods, vocabulary, mathematical structures, and domain foundations.
- **Do:** investigate, build, communicate, use tools, collaborate, and solve problems.
- **Become responsible for:** judgment, values, purpose, accountability, and the consequences of action.

These dimensions reinforce each other. Knowledge without practice can remain inert; practice without understanding can become brittle; capability without responsibility can cause harm.

## 4. What should change in education?

### From memorization alone to usable understanding

Foundational knowledge still matters. The change is to assess whether learners can explain, apply, question, and transfer it—not merely reproduce it.

### From subject silos to connected foundations and deep domains

Learners need shared foundations across evidence, reasoning, mathematics, language, systems, and ethics, while still developing genuine depth in selected fields. Interdisciplinary breadth must not become shallow familiarity with everything.

### From answer production to question formation and verification

AI makes polished answers cheaper. Learning should therefore give greater weight to framing good questions, checking claims, designing tests, interpreting evidence, and explaining why a conclusion is warranted.

### From individual execution to human–agent collaboration

Learners should practice delegating bounded tasks, specifying quality criteria, inspecting intermediate work, and retaining responsibility for integration and decisions. They should also learn when not to use an agent.

### From fixed curricula to navigable learning pathways

A common capability framework can support different routes based on learner goals, prior knowledge, context, and interests. Paths should remain revisable and allow meaningful specialization.

## 5. Human–agent division of work

Do not define the boundary as a permanent list of tasks machines cannot do. Capabilities evolve and overlap. Instead, establish a governance principle:

- People and communities set purposes, priorities, acceptable risks, and legitimate constraints.
- Agents may research, generate alternatives, analyze, simulate, implement, and coordinate within authorized boundaries.
- People must be able to inspect consequential evidence, challenge outputs, understand relevant limitations, and decide whether to rely on results.
- Responsibility for deployment and consequences must be assigned to accountable people or institutions; it must not disappear into an agent workflow.
- For low-risk routine work, oversight may be lightweight. For high-impact work, verification, qualified review, and appeal or rollback mechanisms are required.

Human agency does not require humans to perform every step manually. It requires meaningful capacity to direct, understand, contest, and take responsibility for the system.

## 6. Assessment: measure capability, not polish

A learner's performance should be assessed through evidence such as:

| Capability evidence | What it demonstrates |
|---|---|
| Explanation in the learner's own words | Conceptual understanding |
| A justified choice between alternatives | Judgment and reasoning |
| A tested model, experiment, or artifact | Practical competence |
| Transfer to a new context | Flexible understanding |
| Identification and correction of an error | Calibration and learning |
| A documented agent workflow with verification | Responsible orchestration |
| A novel question or defensible original contribution | Creativity and discovery |
| Reflection on stakeholders and consequences | Ethical and civic judgment |

Do not infer learning from a polished artifact alone. Where an agent contributed, assessment should distinguish what the learner specified, understood, checked, changed, and can independently explain.

## 7. Developmental progression

The framework is not age-banded. A learner may demonstrate advanced creative ability while still developing quantitative foundations, or deep domain expertise while learning agent orchestration.

A useful progression is:

1. **Orient:** recognize concepts, tools, evidence types, and limits.
2. **Explain:** give coherent accounts and connect representations.
3. **Apply:** use methods in familiar situations.
4. **Evaluate:** compare alternatives, test assumptions, and judge evidence.
5. **Transfer:** adapt knowledge to unfamiliar situations.
6. **Create:** formulate new questions and produce original work.
7. **Steward:** consider consequences, maintain quality, mentor others, and improve shared knowledge.

Progression is iterative, not strictly linear. Real projects often require returning to foundations.

## 8. Connection to the six knowledge domains

The capability framework is cross-cutting. No capability belongs exclusively to one domain.

- **Cognitive foundations:** language, learning agility, critical evaluation, self-direction.
- **Evidence and reality:** observation, measurement, epistemic judgment, causal inquiry.
- **Models and principles:** quantitative reasoning, formal reasoning, mental modeling.
- **Systems and interdisciplinary thinking:** problem framing, systems reasoning, transfer.
- **Creativity and discovery:** design, making, imagination, original inquiry.
- **Civilization and values:** ethics, accountability, care, civic and long-horizon judgment.

The domain map organizes knowledge; this framework describes what learners should become able to do with that knowledge.

## 9. Implementation priorities

For the first curriculum cycle, prioritize a small set of integrated capabilities rather than trying to teach all twenty independently:

1. Frame a meaningful question.
2. Find and evaluate evidence.
3. Build and test a model.
4. Reason about uncertainty and causality.
5. Work with an AI agent and verify its contribution.
6. Communicate a justified conclusion.
7. Create or improve something and account for its consequences.

These priorities should be tested through the first flagship tutorials and revised using expert review and learner evidence.

## 10. Open questions requiring research and deliberation

- Which capabilities transfer robustly across age groups, cultures, and domains?
- How should foundational knowledge and AI-mediated retrieval be balanced?
- What assessment methods can distinguish learner understanding from agent-generated performance?
- How do we avoid turning this framework into a narrow productivity or employability checklist?
- How should care, embodied experience, aesthetic judgment, and civic participation be taught and assessed?
- Which capabilities require explicit human practice even when AI can perform the corresponding task?
- How should the framework evolve as AI capabilities and social institutions change?

These questions are part of the curriculum's research agenda, not settled conclusions.
