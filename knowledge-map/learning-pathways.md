# Learning Pathways: Navigate by Questions, Not a Single Ladder

## 1. Why pathways exist

The six knowledge domains are a map, not a compulsory sequence. Learners arrive with different goals, backgrounds, interests, and prior knowledge. A useful pathway begins with a question or intended capability and connects the concepts, practices, and tutorials needed to make progress.

## 2. Common pathway patterns

### A. Understand a phenomenon
1. Describe what is observed.
2. Identify the relevant concepts and representations.
3. Learn the mechanisms and models.
4. compare predictions with evidence.
5. Explain limits and uncertainty.
6. Formulate a better question.

Typical domains: cognitive foundations; evidence and reality; models and principles.

### B. Make a decision
1. Clarify the decision and stakeholders.
2. Identify facts, uncertainties, and value judgments.
3. Compare causal mechanisms and possible outcomes.
4. Examine systems effects and trade-offs.
5. State a justified decision and what could change it.

Typical domains: evidence and reality; systems and interdisciplinary thinking; civilization and values.

### C. Build an artifact
1. Define the need and constraints.
2. Learn the relevant principles and methods.
3. Generate alternative designs.
4. Prototype using suitable tools and agents.
5. Test against explicit criteria.
6. Iterate and communicate limitations.

Typical domains: models and principles; systems and interdisciplinary thinking; creativity and discovery.

### D. Create new knowledge
1. Map what is already known.
2. Identify an unresolved question or gap.
3. Develop competing hypotheses or approaches.
4. Design an investigation or proof strategy.
5. Gather or generate evidence.
6. Critique, reproduce, and revise.
7. Publish a traceable contribution.

Typical domains: all six, depending on the subject.

### E. Interpret a human or cultural question
1. Clarify terms, context, and whose perspective is represented.
2. Examine historical and cultural sources.
3. distinguish empirical claims from interpretation and values.
4. Compare competing frameworks.
5. Evaluate consequences and ethical tensions.
6. Develop a reasoned, revisable position.

Typical domains: cognitive foundations; evidence and reality; systems; civilization and values.

## 3. Pathway metadata

A pathway should record:
- target question or capability;
- learner profile and assumed starting point;
- entry concepts and diagnostic activity;
- essential and optional learning nodes;
- prerequisite relationships and alternative routes;
- practical tasks and assessment evidence;
- estimated effort and accessibility alternatives;
- where agent support is useful and where independent reasoning is required;
- exit capability and suggested next questions.

## 4. Navigation principles

- Offer more than one entry point.
- Distinguish prerequisite from recommended background.
- Allow learners to move sideways across domains.
- Make uncertainty and contested knowledge visible.
- Do not infer mastery from reading or completion alone.
- Recommend the next step based on demonstrated capability, not engagement time alone.
- Keep specialist depth available; interdisciplinary breadth must not become superficiality.

## 5. Initial pathway examples

The first pathway is the Tutorial 01 scientific-thinking investigation. Future pathway examples should include mathematical modeling, evidence evaluation, engineering design, creative production, and ethical decision-making. Each example must link to actual learning assets as they become available; do not invent completed courses.
