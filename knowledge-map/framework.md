# Knowledge Framework: Six Interconnected Domains

This framework describes complementary dimensions of human understanding and capability. It is not a rigid ladder, nor a replacement for disciplinary knowledge.

## 1. Cognitive foundations — learning to represent and reason

**Purpose:** provide shared tools for understanding and communicating.

Includes language and literacy, mathematical reasoning, logic, computation, information literacy, visual and spatial reasoning, and scientific habits of mind.

**Outcomes:** explain concepts clearly; reason from assumptions; use quantitative representations; recognize ambiguity; communicate across formats.

## 2. Evidence and reality — learning what is warranted

**Purpose:** distinguish observation, measurement, interpretation, inference, and speculation.

Includes observation, measurement, data quality, experimental design, sampling, uncertainty, probability, causal inference, reproducibility, source criticism, and research ethics.

**Outcomes:** evaluate evidence; identify limitations and confounders; communicate uncertainty; design a fair test; trace claims to sources.

## 3. Models and principles — learning why things happen

**Purpose:** explain phenomena using mechanisms, laws, theories, and models.

Includes mathematics, physical principles, biological mechanisms, computational models, economic mechanisms, psychological theories, and social explanations.

**Outcomes:** state assumptions; derive or interpret a model; identify its domain of validity; compare predictions with observations; revise explanations when evidence conflicts.

## 4. Systems and interdisciplinary thinking — learning how things connect

**Purpose:** understand interactions, feedback, networks, scale, and emergent behavior.

Includes systems thinking, complexity, networks, control, ecology, institutions, infrastructure, lifecycle thinking, and cross-domain knowledge transfer.

**Outcomes:** map relationships; identify feedback loops and unintended effects; reason across scales; transfer a method while checking whether its assumptions still hold.

## 5. Creativity and discovery — learning to make the not-yet-existing

**Purpose:** develop the ability to formulate valuable questions and produce original, testable, or meaningful outcomes.

Includes creativity methods, scientific discovery, engineering design, artistic practice, entrepreneurship, prototyping, experimentation, collaboration, and human–agent workflows.

**Outcomes:** frame open problems; generate alternatives; prototype; test; iterate; explain what is genuinely new and what remains uncertain.

## 6. Civilization and values — learning what is worth creating

**Purpose:** deliberate about ends, not only means.

Includes philosophy, ethics, history, political and economic institutions, law, culture, governance, sustainability, human flourishing, technology futures, and responsibility.

**Outcomes:** identify stakeholders and value conflicts; reason about consequences; compare institutional choices; justify decisions; remain open to plural perspectives.

## Cross-cutting dimensions

Representations; scale; time; uncertainty; agency; ethics. Every domain should consider these dimensions.

## How to use this framework

Start with a question or phenomenon. Identify the relevant domains and prerequisites. Follow links between concepts rather than forcing the question into one discipline. A learning pathway may enter through any domain and revisit others as needed.
