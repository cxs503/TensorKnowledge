# Knowledge Map

The knowledge map is a navigable model of ideas, practices, evidence, and questions—not a fixed textbook table of contents.

## How to read the map

- **Nodes** represent distinct knowledge objects. A node may be a concept, method, model, claim, experiment, dataset, skill, or question.
- **Domains** are broad perspectives for navigation. A node may belong to more than one domain.
- **Prerequisites** indicate a genuine dependency for understanding or using a node. Do not use them merely to recommend a convenient order.
- **Relations** express other connections, such as "informs", "tests", "uses", or "constrains". Relation labels must be interpreted in context; a link is not evidence that the connected claim is true.
- **Pathways** assemble nodes around a question or capability. They are routes through the map, not the map itself.
- **Tutorials** are learning experiences that teach and assess selected nodes.

## The six domains

1. **Cognitive foundations** — reasoning, attention, memory, learning, language, and judgment.
2. **Evidence and reality** — observation, measurement, data, uncertainty, causality, and methods of inquiry.
3. **Models and principles** — mathematics, logic, scientific models, abstraction, and explanatory principles.
4. **Systems and interdisciplinary thinking** — interactions, feedback, emergence, complexity, and transfer across fields.
5. **Creativity and discovery** — imagination, problem framing, hypothesis generation, design, and creation.
6. **Civilization and values** — ethics, institutions, culture, collective decisions, and long-term consequences.

These domains overlap. They should not be interpreted as six sequential stages or as mutually exclusive academic departments.

## Current map coverage

The initial nodes focus on scientific inquiry and its connections to causal reasoning, systems thinking, mathematical modeling, critical thinking, and ethical reasoning. This is a deliberately small seed graph, not a claim of comprehensive coverage.

## Contribution rules

1. Give every node a stable ID; changing a title does not change identity.
2. Write one primary idea per node. Split concepts that require substantially different learning outcomes.
3. Add prerequisites only for real dependencies.
4. Use relations to express useful connections without implying equivalence or proof.
5. Keep evidence status separate from editorial review status.
6. Add sources for substantive factual claims; do not use an empty source list to imply verification.
7. Run `python scripts/validate_knowledge_assets.py` before proposing a change.
8. A passing structural check does not replace expert review or learner testing.
