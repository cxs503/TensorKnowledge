# Source and Claim Standard

## Purpose

Make it possible for a learner, reviewer, or agent to trace important knowledge claims to the evidence and reasoning that support them.

## 1. Classify claims

For each central claim, identify whether it is:
- empirical observation or measurement;
- mathematical result or derivation;
- established theory or principle;
- interpretation or synthesis;
- hypothesis or proposal;
- normative or value judgment;
- illustrative or simulated example.

Do not present one category as another. A value judgment is not settled by a measurement; a simulation is not an empirical observation.

## 2. Source quality

Prefer sources appropriate to the claim:
- primary research for specific empirical findings;
- systematic reviews or authoritative syntheses for broader evidence;
- textbooks for established foundational explanations;
- standards and official documentation for technical specifications;
- original historical documents and reputable scholarship for historical claims;
- clearly identified perspectives for philosophical and normative arguments.

A source being authoritative does not make every claim within it universally applicable. Check date, scope, method, conflicts, and limitations.

## 3. Required source record

For each important source, capture:
- author or responsible organization;
- exact title;
- publication date and version;
- DOI, stable URL, ISBN, or other persistent identifier;
- source type;
- relevant page, section, table, or figure;
- access date for changeable resources;
- license or reuse constraints where relevant.

## 4. Claim-to-source mapping

A citation should support the claim immediately associated with it. Do not attach a general bibliography to unsupported statements. If a source supports only a narrow scope, state that scope.

Where a claim depends on several sources, explain their respective contributions. Preserve disagreements and contrary evidence.

## 5. Agent research protocol

Agents must:
1. locate the original source where possible;
2. verify title, author, date, and identifier;
3. inspect the relevant passage or data, not only a search snippet;
4. distinguish source statements from the agent's synthesis;
5. record uncertainty, inaccessible sources, and conflicting evidence;
6. never invent citations or claim to have read inaccessible material.

## 6. Publication blockers

Block publication when:
- a central citation cannot be verified;
- a source does not support the attached claim;
- a fabricated or materially misleading reference is found;
- simulated data are presented as observed data;
- an important contested claim is presented as settled without qualification.

## 7. Corrections

When a source is retracted, corrected, superseded, or no longer accessible, record the effect on dependent claims and learning assets. Do not silently remove the provenance trail.
